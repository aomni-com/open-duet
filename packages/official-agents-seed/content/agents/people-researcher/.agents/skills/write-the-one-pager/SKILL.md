# Write the One-Pager

The reader has one minute and a meeting. Everything competes for the same page, so the discipline is what gets left off it.

## What earns a place

In order, and nothing outranks the one above it:

1. **Who they are now** — title, employer, how long, where. The line that orients the reader.
2. **How to reach them** — the address, and how sure the provider is about it.
3. **What they have done** — roles with dates, newest first.
4. **What they say in public** — dated, attributable, and specific enough to open a conversation with.
5. **What is missing** — the fields that came back `unfound`, and where you looked.

A skills list, a follower count, and a company boilerplate paragraph earn nothing. Cut them.

## The three states of any line

Every line on the page is one of these, and the reader can always tell which:

- **Retrieved** — a value with a source behind it. Written plainly.
- **`unfound`** — searched and not established. Written as the word, with a note. Never blank, never omitted, never quietly replaced by something typical for the role.
- **Inference** — a reading of the evidence rather than the evidence. Allowed, and it says so in the sentence: *"the title and the tenure suggest they own the platform team, though nothing retrieved states it."*

The section most likely to break this rule is "what they appear to own", because a title invites a confident paragraph about responsibilities nobody retrieved. Write it in the language of the evidence you have, or write two sentences instead of five.

## Work history

Newest first. `title`, `company`, `start`, `end` exactly as retrieved; an empty date stays empty. Do not compute tenure the dates do not support, and do not fill or explain a gap — a gap is a fact, and the reader can ask about it.

## Contact channels

Carry the provider's verdict through instead of flattening it to "found": `DELIVERABLE` was verified, `HIGH_PROBABILITY` and `CATCH_ALL` were not. Somebody about to send a first-touch email is making a different decision in each case.

A phone number appears only if the user asked for one. If none has been requested, the row says so — that is not a gap in the research, it is a cost nobody chose to spend.

## Public activity

Two to four entries, each one line, each dated, each linking to a page that was actually opened. Prefer what they said themselves over what was written about them. If everything you found is a press release naming them in a list, the honest entry is that they have little public presence — which tells the reader to prefer a warm introduction over a cold email, and is more useful than three padded bullets.

## Ending the page

Close with the gaps, not with a summary. A summary repeats what the reader just read; the gaps tell them what they are walking in without.

Then say to the user, in the chat rather than on the page, what the research could not establish and what it would take to close it — including whether a phone lookup is the thing they want to spend on.
